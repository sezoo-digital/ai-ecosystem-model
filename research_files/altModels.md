### 1. Cognitive & Protocol Architectures (How Systems Act)

These models focus on runtime state, decision loops, memory structures, and protocol boundaries.

#### A. The CoALA Framework (Cognitive Architectures for Language Agents)

- What it modeled: Published by researchers from Princeton, Stanford, and Google DeepMind, CoALA is the most rigorous academic taxonomy to date for AI agents. It maps LLMs into classical cognitive science frameworks (like SOAR and ACT-R), breaking the agent into Working Memory, Episodic Memory (past experiences), Semantic Memory (factual knowledge), Procedural Memory (rules and code), and an Action Execution Engine.
- Insight for our model: It provides a rigorous basis for our State & Persistence Layer, proving that memory is not merely a "database," but an active cognitive boundary that requires distinct governance rules (e.g., who has the right to modify an agent's procedural memory vs. its episodic history).
- Source:
Sumers, T. R., Yao, S., Narasimhan, K., & Griffiths, T. L. (2023). Cognitive Architectures for Language Agents. arXiv preprint arXiv:2309.02427. https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02427

#### B. Lilian Weng’s Autonomous Agent Architecture (OpenAI)
- What it modeled: The canonical four-quadrant decomposition of an agent: Agent Core (LLM) surrounded by Planning (subgoal decomposition, self-reflection), Memory (short-term vs. long-term vector search), and Tool Use (external APIs, calculators, code interpreters).
- Insight for our model: It illustrates why the raw LLM cannot be governed in isolation; governance must attach to the orchestration loops (planning and reflection) that wrap around it.
- Source:
Weng, L. (2023). LLM Powered Autonomous Agents. OpenAI Research Blog. https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2023-06-23-agent/

#### C. Anthropic’s Systems Architecture: MCP & "Building Effective Agents"
- What it modeled:
  - The Model Context Protocol (MCP): A standardized client-server specification decoupling the Host (runtime harness), the Client (protocol negotiator), and the Servers (isolated data/tool repositories).
  - Workflows vs. Autonomous Agents: A spectrum diagram showing when deterministic code (prompt chaining, routing, orchestrator-workers) should constrain the probabilistic model versus when open-ended agency is appropriate.
- Insight for our model: Anthropic explicitly formalized the Harness as an integration gateway, showing that safety is achieved by constraining the protocol boundary (least-privilege tool discovery) rather than just fine-tuning model weights.
- Sources:
Anthropic. (2024). Model Context Protocol Specification. https://modelcontextprotocol.io
Schluntz, E., & Zhang, B. (2024). Building Effective Agents. Anthropic Research. https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents

### 2. Economic & Market Stacks (Where Capital & Value Flow)
These models illustrate margin profiles, compute capital expenditure, commoditization risks, and platform encroachment.
#### A. Andreessen Horowitz (a16z): "Who Owns the Generative AI Platform?"
- What it modeled: A horizontal stack partitioning the market into three tiers: Compute Infrastructure (Hardware/Cloud), Foundation Models (Closed vs. Open weights), and Application Wrappers (B2B/B2C). It famously analyzed where gross margins accumulate and where value leaks.
- Insight for our model: It formalized the financial transaction vectors in our model: the realization that base model providers operate with lower gross margins due to compute costs, forcing them to encroach upstream into applications to capture margin.
- Sources:
Bornstein, M., Appenzeller, G., & Casado, M. (2023). Who Owns the Generative AI Platform? Andreessen Horowitz. https://a16z.com/who-owns-the-generative-ai-platform/
Bornstein, M., & Radovanovic, R. (2023). Emerging Architectures for LLM Applications. Andreessen Horowitz. https://a16z.com/emerging-architectures-for-llm-applications/

#### B. Sequoia Capital: "AI’s $600B Question" & "Act Two"
- What it modeled: A macro-economic balance sheet model comparing GPU CapEx investments (data center infrastructure spend) against actual end-user revenue generated by downstream AI applications.
- Insight for our model: It highlights the immense economic pressure on Enterprise Principals: enterprises are funding astronomical infrastructure bills, which accelerates the demand for autonomous labor (agents) that can generate tangible ROI rather than speculative chat interfaces.
- Sources:
Cahn, D. (2024). AI's $600B Question. Sequoia Capital. https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/ais-600b-question/
Huang, S., & Grady, P. (2023). Generative AI’s Act Two. Sequoia Capital. https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/generative-ai-act-two/

### 3. Regulatory & Supply-Chain Governance Models (Where Liability Sits)
These frameworks track statutory duties, test/evaluation methodologies, and the division of responsibility across the supply chain.
#### A. NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0 & Generative Profile AI 600-1)
- What it modeled: The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s lifecycle framework. It defines the core functions—Govern, Map, Measure, Manage—and maps a detailed taxonomy of AI Actor Tasks across upstream data collection, model training, deployment, and end-use.
- Insight for our model: NIST provides the formal definition of operational controls. Our "Policy Engine" inside the Harness is essentially the software manifestation of NIST's Manage and Measure functions operating at runtime.
- Sources:
National Institute of Standards and Technology. (2023). Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) (NIST SP 1270). https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.1270
National Institute of Standards and Technology. (2024). Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile (NIST AI 600-1). https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1

#### B. Stanford CRFM: Foundation Model Supply Chain & Transparency Index
- What it modeled: The Stanford Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM) created a comprehensive audit taxonomy assessing foundation models across 100 indicators spanning Upstream (data provenance, labor, compute), Model (architecture, evaluations, capabilities), and Downstream (distribution, terms of service, user privacy).
- Insight for our model: It rigorously maps the boundary between the Frontier Model Provider and the downstream deployer, documenting how transparency degrades as assets move down the stack.
- Sources:
Bommasani, R., et al. (2023). The Foundation Model Transparency Index. Stanford Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM), HAI. arXiv preprint arXiv:2310.12941. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12941
Bommasani, R., et al. (2021). On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models. arXiv preprint arXiv:2108.07258.

#### C. Ada Lovelace Institute & EU AI Act Value Chain Governance
- What it modeled: Detailed policy architectures analyzing the allocation of regulatory burdens along the AI value chain. It specifically diagrams the handoff between upstream General Purpose AI (GPAI) providers and downstream deployers, showing how contract law (ToS liability disclaimers) clashes with statutory consumer protections.
- Insight for our model: This work provided the exact conceptual basis for our model's dashed tension arrow: Frontier Model Providers disclaim liability upward, while Enterprise Deployers absorb strict vicarious liability downward.
- Source:
Ada Lovelace Institute. (2023). Keeping foundation models safe: A governance framework for general-purpose AI. https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/report/keeping-foundation-models-safe/

### 4. Cybernetic & Systems Dynamics Models (Control & Feedback)
If you want to understand how a complex system maintains stability in the presence of noise, drift, and rogue agents, the cybernetics tradition is essential.
#### A. Stafford Beer’s Viable System Model (VSM) applied to AI
- What it modeled: A classic cybernetic model from organizational cybernetics showing the five recursive subsystems necessary for any autonomous system to remain viable:
  - System 1 (Operations): The agent executing actions against target APIs.
  - System 2 (Coordination): Protocols dampening oscillation (e.g., rate limits, MCP tool discovery).
  - System 3 (Operational Control & Audit): The internal Harness enforcing budgets, policies, and logging compliance.
  - System 4 (Intelligence & Adaptation): The Frontier LLM scanning context and planning future moves.
  - System 5 (Policy & Purpose): The Human Principal defining statutory intent, ethical rules, and ultimate governance. 
 - Insight for our model: VSM proves that an AI system cannot be governed solely at System 4 (the model's reasoning) or System 1 (the API execution). Stability requires System 3 (the Harness) to act as the audit-and-control governor between human intent (System 5) and probabilistic operations.
 - Foundational Source:
Beer, S. (1972). Brain of the Firm: The Managerial Cybernetics of Organization. Allen Lane / Penguin Press; John Wiley & Sons.
